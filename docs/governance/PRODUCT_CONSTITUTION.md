## 1. Constitutional purpose

This Constitution defines what Provider Mesh is, whom it serves, the outcomes it exists to advance, the authority it may exercise, and the boundaries it must not cross. These provisions govern all lower-level product, policy, data, security, architecture, and implementation decisions.

This Constitution does not prescribe technical components, interfaces, data schemas, integration methods, or implementation sequences. Those matters belong in subordinate project documents and must remain consistent with this Constitution.

## 2. Product identity

### 2.1 Meaning of the name

The name **Provider Mesh** describes what is connected and how those connections relate. It does not define the person seeking help as a provider, reduce the product to a provider directory, or make participating providers the owners of the person's experience.

**Provider** means the people and organizations capable of delivering services or material assistance. A provider is a real service-capable participant, not merely a directory entry, database record, participating member, or health care institution. Information maintained about providers is a representation of the service ecosystem; it is not the ecosystem itself.

**Mesh** means the evolving many-to-many fabric of purpose-bound relationships among people, providers, supporters, and collaborations. Those relationships may cross domains and organizational boundaries while the participants retain their independent authority, responsibilities, and systems of record. The Mesh may be centrally stewarded without becoming a single institutional authority over every relationship.

The Mesh is therefore not the provider dictionary or any particular representation of provider information. Those are supporting resources that help the Mesh recognize providers, discover services, and sustain accountable connections. The person is why the Mesh exists; the provider is what the Mesh connects the person to; the governed relationship among them is the Mesh.

### 2.2 Mission

Provider Mesh helps people connect to needed services through governed discovery, responsible referrals, and authorized collaboration across the social determinants of health.

### 2.3 Product definition

Provider Mesh is a shared service environment for:

1. understanding a person's expressed need sufficiently to assist;
2. discovering credible and relevant service options;
3. supporting an authorized connection to a selected provider;
4. supporting accountable coordination among people and organizations when more than one participant is involved; and
5. learning, under separate and appropriate authority, from patterns in actual needs, connections, barriers, and outcomes.

Its primary value is not possession of a comprehensive directory. Its value is the ability to respond to a real need with current evidence, a credible next step, continuity where justified, and clear responsibility for what is and is not known.

### 2.4 Desired end state

Provider Mesh succeeds when a person facing a material human need can reach an appropriate source of help with less fragmentation, while retaining agency, confidentiality, and a meaningful route to correction. Providers and collaborations must be able to work across organizational boundaries without surrendering their distinct authority or falsely representing activity, acceptance, or outcomes that the Mesh cannot observe.

The ultimate outcome is a human connection to needed service, not merely the production of information, a search result, a referral record, or a coordination artifact.

## 3. The problem Provider Mesh addresses

People seeking help encounter an ecosystem divided among organizations with different missions, eligibility rules, geographic boundaries, intake practices, operating hours, capacities, and information systems. Published provider descriptions may identify possible resources while leaving current access, suitability, eligibility, or availability uncertain. Information can be incomplete, contradictory, stale, or expressed in terms unfamiliar to the person seeking help.

The same person may work with several providers, use different identifiers, change contact methods, and participate in more than one coordinated effort. No single provider necessarily has a complete or current account of the person's goals, relationships, referrals, or progress. At the same time, centralizing every detail would create unnecessary risk and could displace the person's control.

Provider Mesh addresses this fragmentation by governing how needs are understood, how service options are discovered and represented, how authorized connections are made, and how collaboration and learning occur without merging the independent responsibilities of the people and organizations involved.

## 4. Mission scope and relevance

### 4.1 Mission domain

The Mesh serves essential human needs and material barriers to stability across the social determinants of health, including:

- economic stability;
- education access and quality;
- health care access and quality;
- housing, transportation, safety, and other neighborhood or living conditions; and
- social and community context.

Health care, homelessness, employment, education, food, benefits, legal assistance, transportation, safety, and social support may all fall within the mission when they concern a material human need or barrier. The Mesh is not limited to organizations traditionally described as health care providers.

### 4.2 Context determines relevance

Relevance is determined by the person's circumstances and the purpose of the assistance, not by keywords alone. An ordinary consumer request may be outside the mission, while a similar-sounding request may become relevant because it concerns health, safety, subsistence, education, employment, or access to essential services.

For example, an ordinary request for a manicure is normally outside the mission. Qualified foot care for a person with diabetes may concern health access. Training as a nail technician may concern education and employment. The Mesh must not manufacture a social-needs rationale merely because it has the capability to answer a request.

### 4.3 Individual and collective scope

At the individual level, the Mesh supports a person in reaching needed help. At the collective level, separately authorized learning and collaboration may address recurring barriers or a defined common agenda.

A collaboration may establish a narrower focus, such as homelessness, poverty, education, health, or addiction. That collaboration's boundary does not become the universal boundary of the Mesh. Participation in one collaboration does not automatically enroll a person or provider in another.

### 4.4 Requesters

A request may originate from:

- the person who has the need;
- a family member, navigator, caseworker, guardian, legal representative, or other supporter;
- a provider or collaboration participant; or
- an authorized automated requester acting for a person or organization.

The requester and the person with the need are not necessarily the same. The Mesh must establish the authority appropriate to the requested action. A requester's role, organizational membership, possession of an identifier, or technical access does not by itself establish permission to view, disclose, or act upon a person's information.

## 5. Constitutional definitions

For this Constitution:

- **Person** means the human whose need, service connection, information, or outcome is at issue. The person remains the intended beneficiary even when someone else makes the request.
- **Requester** means the person, supporter, organization, or authorized automated actor asking the Mesh to provide assistance or perform an action.
- **Provider** means a real person or organization capable of offering a service or material assistance. A provider exists independently of any record or description of it.
- **Mesh** means the evolving many-to-many fabric of purpose-bound relationships among people, providers, supporters, and collaborations. It is not synonymous with a directory, database, membership list, or single technical connection method.
- **Discovery** means the need-directed process of identifying and evaluating potentially relevant providers, services, requirements, and access pathways.
- **Evidence** means information supporting a claim, together with its source, time, limitations, and applicable authority.
- **Referral** means an authorized, bounded request to a provider for consideration of a service connection for a person.
- **Connection** means a communication or handoff between relevant parties. It does not by itself establish acceptance or service receipt.
- **Outcome** means a reported or observed event following discovery or referral, such as contact, acceptance, service receipt, an unmet need, or another barrier. Each outcome is a distinct claim requiring an appropriate basis.
- **Collaboration** means a governed relationship among multiple participants pursuing an agreed agenda. Shared use of the Mesh, a referral, or a case discussion alone does not necessarily create a formal collaboration.
- **Backbone role** means the accountable human and organizational work of convening participants, sustaining agreements, coordinating distinct contributions, supporting communication, and enabling shared learning.
- **Platform steward** means the organization accountable for the fair and responsible operation of the shared Provider Mesh environment. Platform stewardship does not confer authority over provider decisions or a person's information beyond the steward's legitimate duties.

## 6. Constitutional articles

The following articles are non-negotiable product rules.

### Article I — Human need and agency are primary

1. The person's need, goals, preferences, and material circumstances must guide the assistance.
2. Institutional goals, available programs, or technical convenience must not silently replace the person's stated priorities.
3. The Mesh may identify related risks, needs, or foreseeable barriers and explain why they matter. It must preserve the person's ability to decline, defer, or reprioritize assistance unless a legitimate legal or safety authority requires otherwise.
4. A person's choice to pursue one option does not authorize the Mesh to treat every related issue as an active request.
5. Consequential action on a person's behalf requires authority proportionate to that action. Human involvement must be proportionate to consequence.

### Article II — Discovery is primary

1. The Mesh must respond to actual human needs by discovering relevant services and evidence.
2. Useful service must not depend on first completing or maintaining a comprehensive directory of the service ecosystem.
3. Discovery must seek information sufficient to support a credible next step, including material access requirements when they are available.
4. Previously encountered providers may support continuity and recognition, but a new inquiry must not represent historical observations as current without appropriate current evidence.
5. A provider can be considered during discovery whether or not it is a participating member of the Mesh.

### Article III — Representations must remain accountable to evidence

1. No directory, publication, provider, institution, or other source is presumed authoritative for every claim.
2. Authority is claim-specific. Evidence appropriate for legal identity may differ from evidence appropriate for licensing, service capability, eligibility, current availability, referral acceptance, or outcome.
3. Consequential claims must retain identifiable sources and meaningful time information.
4. The Mesh must distinguish information that is known, reported, inferred, conflicting, stale, or unconfirmed.
5. Classification, summarization, repetition, or confident wording must not increase a claim's apparent certainty beyond its evidence.
6. Conflicts must be disclosed, responsibly reconciled, or escalated. They must not be silently overwritten.
7. A recommendation must remain explainable in terms of the person's need and the evidence used.

### Article IV — Natural language is open; assistance is mission-bounded

1. People may describe needs in their own words, including through statements that do not take the form of explicit requests.
2. The Mesh may recognize a possible need, acknowledge it, and ask clarifying questions when missing context could materially change the help offered.
3. Clarification must be limited to what is reasonably needed to understand the need, identify relevant options, or support an authorized handoff. It must not become unnecessary intake or an indefinite substitute for human services.
4. Once sufficient understanding exists, the Mesh must move toward relevant service choices and, when requested and authorized, a responsible connection.
5. Taxonomies and shared vocabularies may support interpretation and comparison. They must not erase material context, force a person's experience into an unsuitable category, or define the entire universe of legitimate help.
6. Basic discovery must not be conditioned on permanent identification, optional information sharing, possession of conventional identity evidence, or the person's independent ability to use digital technology.
7. People facing disability, language, literacy, technology, identity, communication, or similar access barriers must have a route to appropriate human assistance or an accessible alternative.
8. A provider may impose legitimate identification or eligibility requirements for its own service. The Mesh must distinguish those provider requirements from access to the Mesh itself and disclose them before a consequential handoff when reasonably knowable.

### Article V — Capability is subordinate to purpose and permission

1. The ability to obtain information, infer a condition, make a match, contact a party, or support an action does not establish that doing so is relevant, authorized, or appropriate.
2. The Mesh must not create consent, professional judgment, provider commitment, eligibility, identity certainty, or outcome certainty through inference.
3. AI and human operators may exercise disciplined initiative only within the approved mission, protected rights, evidence duties, and legitimate authority.
4. Initiative may include seeking stronger evidence, adapting when help is unavailable, reconsidering a need with the person, and revisiting an unsuccessful connection.
5. Initiative must not invent facts, conceal uncertainty, override the person's priorities without legitimate authority, or assume a provider's responsibilities.

### Article VI — Continuity must be proportionate and identity must remain contestable

1. General discovery must remain available without permanent identification when identity is unnecessary to provide useful assistance.
2. The Mesh may establish a continuing personal relationship and assign a Mesh continuity identifier when needed for a requested service, authorized coordination, accountability, or continuity.
3. A provisional personal relationship may be established when complete conventional identity information is unavailable. Reported first name, last name, and date of birth may provide a default threshold for an asserted identity, but they do not prove identity, guarantee uniqueness, or justify an automatic record merge.
4. Identifiers assigned by homeless-services, health, education, benefits, justice, or other provider systems may be associated with the same person under an appropriate level of validation. No external system or domain identifier is universally canonical for the Mesh.
5. The personal relationship may be capable of representing information required by relevant service-domain standards, including the full HMIS Universal Data Element set. Capability to represent an element does not require universal collection, completion, disclosure, or retention of that element.
6. Identity evidence, demographic information, changing circumstances, project or service participation, and longitudinal history are distinct kinds of information. Each must retain appropriate source, time, quality, authority, and dispute information.
7. Continuity does not justify a universal personal dossier. Additional information must be collected at the time and for the purpose it is needed.
8. Unstable phone access, changing email, lack of a personal device, or use of a shared or public device must not erase a person's continuity or automatically disqualify the person from assistance.
9. A possible identity match must not be treated as proven sameness without sufficient validation for the consequence involved. Recognition of a person does not itself authorize disclosure or action.
10. A person must have a meaningful way to challenge and correct a mistaken association.

### Article VII — Trusted relationships carry bounded authority

1. A person may authorize others to assist, including family members, navigators, caseworkers, providers, and other supporters.
2. Voluntary delegation, professional responsibility, custodial responsibility, fiduciary authority, guardianship, legal representation, and compulsory relationships are distinct forms of authority and must not be collapsed into a single trusted status.
3. Authority must be limited by purpose, information, action, duration, and applicable law or agreement.
4. Membership, employment, professional standing, participation in a collaboration, or knowledge of a person's identifier does not confer blanket access.
5. The Mesh must comply with each applicable legal, contractual, professional, and institutional obligation while preserving the person's agency to the greatest lawful extent and limiting disclosure or intervention to the authorized purpose.
6. No automatic hierarchy among health, homeless-services, education, justice, or other domain rules may be presumed. Where authorities conflict or their application is materially uncertain, the Mesh must not silently choose among them; the conflict must receive accountable human review.
7. The person must have a meaningful route to explanation, objection, correction, reconsideration, and escalation, subject to legitimate limits on the person's authority.

### Article VIII — Confidentiality follows information

1. Nonpublic personal information must receive a strong common baseline of confidentiality across service domains.
2. Equal protection does not mean equal access. Access must remain limited to a legitimate purpose and appropriate authority.
3. The person's rights, the contributor's responsibilities, and the recipient's custody obligations are distinct and continue when information is summarized, combined, or shared.
4. Permission to share one account, document, or fact does not authorize disclosure of all related information.
5. A membership agreement, consent, or collaboration charter must not be interpreted as unrestricted authority beyond its stated purpose and scope.
6. Applicable legal and professional duties continue to apply. This Constitution establishes product principles; it does not by itself establish legal compliance.
7. Withdrawal, correction, retention, and continuing confidentiality obligations require accountable reconciliation. They must not be reduced to an assumption that one participant owns the complete account.

### Article IX — Referrals are bounded handoffs, not guaranteed outcomes

1. A referral must describe the person's relevant need, the desired service connection, the authority for the referral, and information reasonably necessary for the provider to consider it.
2. The Mesh is accountable for its representations and handoff actions.
3. The receiving provider remains responsible for its own eligibility, acceptance, professional, service, and recordkeeping decisions.
4. A provider's acceptance must be explicit or supported by appropriate evidence. It must not be created by an AI summary, a requester's expectation, or transmission of a referral.
5. Discovery, recommendation, attempted contact, successful contact, referral, provider acceptance, enrollment, service receipt, and satisfaction of need are different claims.
6. A responsible handoff does not automatically establish that the service was received or the need was resolved.
7. Activity outside the Mesh remains unknown unless a person with appropriate knowledge and authority reports it or another appropriate source establishes it.
8. Every inquiry or referral must reach a defined procedural disposition based on what the Mesh can establish. A procedural disposition may record that information was provided, a referral was transmitted, a response was received, no response was observed, the person declined further action, contact was lost, or another appropriate state occurred. Procedural closure must not be represented as resolution of the person's need.
9. The Mesh does not promise a provider response or a successful outcome. It must accurately state what it did, what remains unknown, and whether any further Mesh responsibility has been accepted.
10. When available information indicates a credible immediate and serious threat to health or safety, the Mesh must direct the requester to an appropriate emergency, crisis, or specialized provider and facilitate contact when authorized and available. Urgent direction must not await routine Springboard platform review, and general information alone must not be represented as sufficient handling of the threat.
11. Referral transmission or provider acceptance does not itself enroll the person, bind the person to provider terms, or transfer complete ownership of the referral account or related information.
12. A provider receiving referral information assumes custody obligations for the information it receives and responsibility for records it creates. The Mesh and requester retain responsibility for their own legitimate records, and the person's continuing rights remain applicable across those records.
13. A referral may lead to continuing one-to-one coordination. It may develop into a multi-provider collaboration only when the additional participants, purpose, authority, and governing agreement are established.

### Article X — Provider identity is independent of representation or membership

1. Discovering or retaining information about a provider does not establish membership, endorsement, referral acceptance, or authority to disclose personal information to that provider.
2. A participating provider may take responsibility for information it contributes, but its statements do not become universally conclusive merely because it participates.
3. Participation does not make all provider information current and does not grant ownership of independent evidence, another participant's account, or the person's complete record.
4. The extent of each provider's contribution, asserted authority, and maintenance responsibility must remain explicit.
5. The Mesh must not preferentially represent a provider merely because that provider participates or because the platform steward also operates services.

### Article XI — Collaborations are governed by shared agendas, not platform proximity

1. Multiple providers discussing or coordinating assistance for the same person may form a collaboration when they deliberately establish a shared purpose and appropriate authority.
2. A formal collaboration must be established through the express agreement of participating organizations acting through appropriate authority. Its charter or equivalent agreement must define its common agenda, population or problem focus, commitments, decision rights, shared understanding of progress, information boundaries, conflict process, and backbone mandate.
3. Collaboration participants may retain different case-management approaches, professional duties, and organizational responsibilities.
4. Shared participation does not merge provider authority, create exclusive control over a person, automatically enroll other clients, or carry authority and information from one collaboration into another.
5. Collective goals must not override client rights or authorize unrestricted disclosure.
6. Collaboration legitimacy arises from participating organizations and meaningful involvement of the people or communities affected, not from software alone.
7. Collective Impact principles—including a common agenda, shared measurement, mutually reinforcing activities, continuous communication, and backbone support—may guide collaboration. Use of a common environment does not establish those conditions automatically.
8. This Constitution governs the shared baseline of client rights, confidentiality, evidence integrity, and platform conduct. A collaboration charter governs the collaboration's agenda and activities only within that baseline.
9. Overlapping collaborations must not automatically exchange information, authority, enrollment, or credit for outcomes. Conflicts are handled first through the applicable charters and then through platform governance when shared constitutional obligations or platform integrity are implicated.

### Article XII — Backbone and platform authority remain distinct

1. Backbone work is an accountable human and organizational responsibility. Software may support but does not replace convening, agreement, judgment, communication, or accountability.
2. Each collaboration must identify the organization entrusted with its backbone role. Springboard or another organization may serve in that role.
3. Springboard's stewardship of Provider Mesh is distinct from Springboard's role as a service provider and from any backbone role assigned to it by a collaboration.
4. Authority, access, or information entrusted to Springboard in one role must not automatically extend to another role.
5. Platform stewardship must be impartial and answerable. It must not confer preferential referral placement or privileged use of participant information for Springboard's own services.
6. Conflicts of interest must be disclosed and addressed through accountable review.
7. Springboard Delaware is the legal platform operator and steward unless that responsibility is changed through an approved constitutional amendment and accountable transfer plan. Springboard's Board is the institutional governing authority and may delegate product and operational authority through its governing documents and formal actions.
8. Financial support does not by itself grant a funder constitutional authority, ownership of participant information, or control of referral results.
9. Platform governance must provide meaningful participation by affected people and participating organizations proportionate to the decisions being made.
10. Stewardship includes continuity obligations for leadership change, funding interruption, transfer, suspension, or closure of the platform. A change in steward must not silently extinguish existing confidentiality, custody, correction, or accountability duties.

### Article XIII — Persistence is selective and purpose-limited

1. Information may be retained only for justified service access, authorized continuity, accountability, legal or operational duties, or separately permitted learning.
2. A useful relationship or technically available fact does not justify continual collection of every potentially relevant detail.
3. Retention must remain proportionate to purpose, sensitivity, consequence, and the person's reasonable expectations.
4. The minimum information sufficient for one action may be insufficient for a more consequential action. Additional validation or information must be sought when the consequence requires it, not collected universally in anticipation of every possible future use.
5. A continuing identity relationship may be durable without making the complete personal record permanent. Identity links, permissions, referral records, contributed documents, changing circumstances, outcome claims, and audit evidence may require different retention periods and treatment.
6. Changing facts must be capable of retaining their historical source and effective time rather than being silently overwritten as though the earlier account never existed.
7. Expiration or withdrawal of permission must end future access or use to the extent required by the governing authority. It does not falsify history or automatically recall information already received under legitimate authority.
8. Correction may require amendment, annotation, supersession, or separation of a mistaken identity link rather than silent deletion. Confidentiality and accountable custody may survive expiration of permission or departure from the Mesh.

### Article XIV — Learning must be authorized and proportionate to evidence

1. Reported referral experiences and access outcomes may improve future discovery, reveal barriers, and support agreed collective action.
2. Incomplete, historical, or disputed outcomes must not permanently determine a provider's capability or a person's future prospects.
3. Operational use of information to assist a person and secondary use for population analysis, research, model development, commercial purposes, eligibility judgment, risk scoring, or enforcement are distinct purposes. Authority for the first does not automatically authorize the second.
4. De-identification and aggregation do not eliminate the need to consider what can still be inferred about a person, group, or community.
5. Collective analysis must distinguish among service demand, attempted access, successful connection, coordination activity, individual outcome, and population change.
6. Participation gaps, reporting gaps, and uncertainty must remain part of the account.
7. Shared measurement requires agreed meaning. Overlapping collaborations must not claim independent credit for the same improvement without an evidence-based attribution method.
8. The value of learning does not by itself justify additional collection or a use that could disadvantage the people the Mesh exists to assist.
9. Longitudinal population analysis and research are permitted secondary purposes only under a Board-approved policy requiring defined public or client benefit, proportionate collection, appropriate privacy protection, accountable oversight, and compliance with applicable authority. Identifiable research requires specific authorization beyond ordinary service use and independent legal and privacy review.
10. Springboard may develop and evaluate bounded Mesh instructions, classifications, retrieval methods, and decision-support logic using public, synthetic, or appropriately de-identified information under approved controls. Identifiable or confidential personal information must not be used to train a general-purpose or third-party model. Any other training use of Mesh-derived personal information is prohibited unless authorized through a constitutional amendment.
11. Sale of personal information, behavioral advertising based on personal needs, and punitive profiling are prohibited. Any exception requires a constitutional amendment rather than a lower-level policy decision.
12. Access to basic Mesh discovery is not an eligibility determination. The Mesh may communicate or help evaluate published provider requirements but must not substitute its own judgment for a provider's eligibility decision.
13. The Mesh may enforce its own access, confidentiality, and participation rules. It has no general governmental, criminal-justice, or provider enforcement authority.

### Article XV — Growth must remain useful, open, and honest

1. The Mesh must provide useful assistance before every relevant provider participates or every possible connection is available.
2. The network may grow through actual needs, repeated discovery, provider participation, referrals, and collaborations.
3. Incomplete participation and coverage must remain explicit.
4. Technical or organizational maturation may expand available pathways without changing the person's constitutional protections or falsely implying universal coverage.

### Article XVI — Accountability, correction, and remedy remain human obligations

1. Consequential recommendations, identity associations, disclosures, referrals, summaries, and outcome claims must remain attributable to accountable people and institutions.
2. AI-generated or AI-derived accounts must remain identifiable as such, connected to their sources, and subject to human review and correction.
3. Narrative and structured accounts may complement one another. Neither may omit material context, disagreement, the person's perspective, or the basis for reported progress when those matters affect understanding.
4. AI must not independently create professional judgment, consent, delegated authority, provider commitment, or a confirmed outcome.
5. A person must have a meaningful route to question an account, correct material error, and seek review or remedy.
6. Responsibility for a harmful error must not be assigned to AI as though AI were an accountable institution.
7. The platform steward is accountable for Mesh-generated representations, identity associations, access controls, platform disclosures, and the operation of correction and complaint processes.
8. A contributing participant remains accountable for the authority and accuracy of information it contributes. A receiving provider remains accountable for its intake, eligibility, acceptance, professional decisions, service delivery, disclosures, and provider-created records.
9. A collaboration's lead agency is accountable for the process and obligations assigned to it by the collaboration charter. No lead agency acquires general responsibility for acts outside that mandate.
10. Harm involving more than one participant requires a coordinated response; organizational boundaries must not be used to prevent investigation, correction, notice, or an available remedy.
11. Corporate form, insurance, contracting, and delegation must not displace functional accountability or the person's route to remedy.
12. A material conflict among constitutional obligations requires an accountable human decision and must not be resolved silently through lower-level policy or implementation.

### Article XVII — Human escalation follows accountable authority

1. Human escalation means transferring a matter from automated handling to the person or institution holding the relevant authority. It does not automatically require intervention by Springboard staff.
2. The person or requester retains authority over goals, preferences, and voluntary choices. A provider retains authority over its eligibility, intake, professional, and service decisions. A collaboration retains only the authority established by its charter. Springboard retains authority over operation, access, accountability, and governance of the Mesh.
3. Springboard must provide accountable human review for identity-association disputes, access and permission conflicts, correction requests, complaints concerning Mesh-generated representations, provider-representation disputes, suspected misuse, platform account problems, and privacy or security incidents attributable to the Mesh.
4. Platform human review may be immediate, scheduled, or asynchronous according to the consequence and the published operating commitment. The Mesh must accurately disclose the availability, hours, and expected response time of staffed support.
5. This Constitution does not create a continuous, immediate, or twenty-four-hour Springboard navigation, case-management, clinical, legal, crisis-counseling, or emergency-response duty. Any staffed service commitment must be separately authorized, assigned, resourced, and accurately disclosed.
6. When Springboard provides navigation, case management, or another direct human service, it acts in a separately defined provider role. Authority or information entrusted to that provider role must not automatically extend to Springboard's platform-steward role.
7. Referral follow-up remains with the person, requester, provider, or collaboration participant that accepted it, except to the extent the Mesh or Springboard expressly accepts a defined follow-up responsibility.

## 7. Product boundaries and prohibitions

Provider Mesh is not and must not become:

1. a comprehensive replica of Findhelp, 211, or another service directory;
2. a general shopping, entertainment, or lifestyle concierge;
3. a product limited only to health care or homelessness;
4. a substitute for a provider's professional judgment, eligibility decisions, intake duties, service delivery, or internal recordkeeping;
5. an autonomous clinical, legal, case-management, or crisis-counseling service;
6. an authority that creates consent, identity certainty, provider acceptance, commitments, or outcomes through inference;
7. a universal personal dossier assembled because information might someday be useful;
8. a mechanism for moving information or authority silently between providers, collaborations, programs, or Springboard roles;
9. a guarantee that a listed service is available, a provider will accept a referral, or a person's need will be met; or
10. a substitute for human responsibility when urgent safety, professional judgment, legal authority, or institutional accountability is required.

## 8. Constitutional interpretation

1. **Human benefit governs interpretation.** When provisions can reasonably be read in more than one way, the interpretation that best advances appropriate human help while preserving agency, confidentiality, evidence integrity, and legitimate authority controls.
2. **Uncertainty must be preserved.** Unknown, conflicting, time-sensitive, or unobserved facts must remain visibly uncertain until appropriate evidence supports a stronger claim.
3. **Specific authority is not general authority.** Permission for one purpose, relationship, collaboration, or item of information must not be generalized beyond its scope.
4. **The more consequential the action, the stronger the required authority and evidence.** Low-friction assistance is appropriate when consequences are low; additional verification and human review are required as disclosure, commitment, safety, legal, or service consequences increase.
5. **Lower-level documents may operationalize but not redefine this Constitution.** Policy, security, data, architecture, workflow, and implementation documents must resolve details consistently with these provisions.
6. **Silence is not authorization.** A capability, use, or transfer not addressed here must be evaluated against purpose, rights, evidence, authority, and applicable law before it is treated as permitted.

## 9. Governance and amendment

### 9.1 Approval

Only the designated human decision authority may approve this Constitution. Approval must be explicit and recorded in a distinct approved artifact with:

- an approved version number;
- approver name and authority;
- approval date;
- SHA-256 digest;
- canonical repository path; and
- canonical repository reference.

No discussion, drafting activity, file placement, repeated wording, technical implementation, or apparent consensus substitutes for approval.

### 9.2 Amendment

A proposed change is constitutional when it materially changes the product's identity, mission, intended beneficiary, scope, non-negotiable rights, institutional authority, information-use boundaries, stewardship obligations, or prohibitions.

Constitutional changes require explicit approval by the human decision authority and a new approved version. Lower-level documents and implementations may not amend this Constitution by implication.

### 9.3 Escalation

Work must stop for a human decision when a lower-level requirement or proposed implementation materially conflicts with this Constitution, when two constitutional duties point to materially different actions, or when a consequential use falls outside approved authority.

### 9.4 Required subordinate controls

Before production use involving personal information, consequential referrals, provider participation, or formal collaboration, Springboard must approve and maintain subordinate controls governing:

- identity resolution, assurance, correction, and de-linking;
- privacy, consent, authority, access, disclosure, retention, and audit;
- referral custody, procedural disposition, follow-up, and escalation;
- provider participation and responsibility for contributed information;
- collaboration charters, conflicts, shared measurement, and backbone duties;
- research, longitudinal analysis, model development, and secondary use;
- complaints, incidents, cross-participant investigation, and remedy; and
- continuity, transfer, suspension, and closure of the platform.

These controls may define procedures and assignments but must not weaken or redefine this Constitution.
